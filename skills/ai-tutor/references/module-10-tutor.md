# Module 10 — how to tutor it

Hand-written. The module's own words are in `module-10.md`, generated from the website; this
file is how to run them as a conversation.

**This module numbers its exercises two ways.** Six are *Try it yourself* — 10.1, 10.2, 10.3,
10.4, 10.6, 10.8 — and four are **Decide** — 10.5, 10.7, 10.9, 10.10. The four decisions are the
module. The six exercises exist to make them answerable. The checklist at the end lists all ten,
with the four decisions in bold.

## The shape: an hour of looking, and four sentences written down

Nothing is built. There is no terminal, no folder, no project. What the learner leaves with is
one page holding four decisions — and the page is worth more to their school than any software,
*because the software assumes it.*

| Part | Exercises | Where | What you can do there |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Three cases** | the held question, 10.1 to 10.3 | at school, asking colleagues | set each one up, then wait — these take days, not minutes |
| **Source of truth** | 10.4, **Decide 10.5** | their systems | help count; refuse to decide |
| **Enter vs. view** | 10.6, **Decide 10.7** | their sheets | sort them with them |
| **Stable id** | 10.8, **Decide 10.9** | their SIS | the one place you can give a near-answer |
| **Cut-over** | **Decide 10.10** | a conversation with someone else | the honest wall |
| **One page** | section 10 | conversation | draft it from their four answers |

**The three cases are homework, not chat.** 10.1 needs two colleagues, 10.2 needs them to go and
actually time themselves, 10.3 needs last year's placement file. A learner who does all three
properly will not finish this module in one sitting, and that's fine — the page says "about an
hour, plus some conversations". Offer the stopping points: after 10.3, and after 10.9.

If they want to do it all now from memory, let them, and say what it costs: the reveals in
sections 2 to 4 only land if they actually asked the colleagues. 10.1's reveal is literally
*"either you got three different answers, or you got the same answer and then discovered the
copies don't match"* — there's nothing to reveal if nobody was asked.

## The fence, lighter here than in Module 9

This module needs no student data at all. Two places to watch:

- **10.4** counts the *places* a parent's email lives. Not the addresses. A number is the answer.
- **10.8** has them find two children whose names collide. **They don't need to tell you the
  names** — "two in grade 6" is the whole finding. Say so before they answer, not after.

Everything else is about systems, sheets and who decides.

## Working out where they are

Ask which of the four decisions they've written down. That's the only state that matters, and the
page tells them to write them somewhere. If they've written none, start at 10.1.

## Part A — the three cases

**Opening.** Two short messages. Be clear about what's different: **the only module where you
won't build anything**, and it's deliberate — every module after it assumes these decisions exist,
*and if they don't, what you build will be a faster way to be wrong.*

The held question: *"Your school already holds everything it needs to answer 'how is this child
doing.' So why can't anyone answer it?"* Hold it, don't answer it.

**10.1 — the day the schedule changed.** Tell the case first: 7:50 on a testing morning, three
people answer, two are wrong, and nobody was careless — **they were reading different copies.**
Then the exercise: where did the authoritative schedule for their last special day live? One
line, written down, **then ask two colleagues without showing them the answer.**

When they come back, the reveal, and the first sentence matters as much as the second:
**nobody did anything wrong.** Every copy had a good reason behind it. *The copies are not the
failure.* The failure is that **no one ever said which copy wins** — so every copy is equally
entitled to be believed.

**10.2 — is room 12 free?** Pick a real room and a real time tomorrow, find out definitively,
**and time it.** Note how many places they looked and whether they ended up asking a person.

The reveal is the subtle one and worth drawing out rather than stating: in case 1 there were four
copies of a fact; here **nobody can write the fact down at all.** "Room 12 is free at 10:30" is
the *absence* of bookings, across three systems, at one moment. The only honest way to know it is
to compute it from what *is* booked. And if they asked a person: **they were running the
calculation in somebody's head, because no system was doing it.**

**10.3 — which placement list is final?** Count the versions, including the ones in email, then
answer: **what made one of them final?**

Two failures in one file, and keep them separate because they have different answers:

- **Nothing inside a document can make it authoritative.** Not a filename, not a version number,
  not FINAL in capitals. *If they cannot name the moment a version became final, none of them is
  final — they're files with opinions in the filename.*
- **"M. Cohen" was never a child.** It's a string that happens to match two of them. A person can
  usually work it out from context, *most of the time*. A program cannot, and neither can a
  person who wasn't in the room.

**Section 5 — the joint.** Don't rush past it. The three cases are **one failure in three
costumes**, and the failure is: **nobody decided.** Not "nobody built the right system", not
"nobody bought the right software". Somebody had to say which schedule wins, that free-ness is
calculated rather than written, that this file is now the placement — and nobody did, so everyone
resolved it privately, differently, every time.

## Part B — the three disciplines and the four decisions

**Discipline 1, then 10.4 and Decide 10.5.** A hub is a list of facts about people, and the
single biggest obstacle is that the facts get duplicated. Lists get copied, and **they drift** —
the correction reaches one of ten places, and staff start asking *what was the spreadsheet with
the accurate addresses called?*

10.4: count where a parent's email lives. **Most people stop counting at four.** Then **Decide
10.5** — name, in one written line, the source of truth for **which children are enrolled** —
and then go and check whether it's accurate.

Two things to say here, and the second is the one they'll remember:

- **It is a decision, not a discovery.** No file becomes authoritative by being correct; it
  becomes authoritative because somebody with the standing to say so said it, **out loud, on a
  date.**
- Most people find the thing they named **is not the thing anybody actually uses** — and the
  page is right that this is a more useful finding than getting it right would have been. Say so
  *before* they check, so the awkward answer feels like the point rather than a failure.

**Don't make this decision for them.** You can help them see which candidate is accountable; you
cannot name their school's source of truth, and a tutor who offers one has made the module's own
mistake.

**Discipline 2, then 10.6 and Decide 10.7.** *Entered* sources are where information is created
— often ugly, dense, hard to read, **and that's fine, because their main readers are programs.**
*Views* are built from them: generated, never maintained. **You don't update a view; you rebuild
it.**

The generous part of this discipline is worth saying out loud, because it changes how people hear
it: all those private spreadsheets exist because the official sheet was unreadable, **and they
weren't wrong to want something readable.** The answer isn't to forbid it. **Everybody can have
the view they want, as long as nobody is typing into it.**

10.6: name one sheet they maintain by hand that's really a view, and — the good question —
**how would they know if it had gone stale?** Then Decide 10.7: three sheets their team relies
on, each marked *entered* or *view*. **A sheet that's both is the one to fix first**, and now
they know why.

**Discipline 3, then 10.8 and Decide 10.9.** A name is not an identity — *a label that is usually
unique and changes over time.* The hub's rule: as far as it's concerned, **a student does not
exist unless they have a stable id**, and the id says nothing descriptive. No age, no grade, no
name. That's the point.

The registry is **two files, not one** — a roster with one row per child, and a names file with
one row per name a child is known by. Give the table. And the thing people get wrong first: **do
not put aliases in a single cell.** It looks tidy and is unusable.

Aside 1 is worth quoting nearly whole, because it's a rule people break the first week:
**`student_names.csv` is append-only.** New spelling, new row. Never edit, never delete. *Delete
"Rebeca Miller" and every historical file with that spelling stops resolving — years of her
scores quietly disappear from her page, with nothing to tell you it happened.*

10.8: two children whose names collide, or one whose name changed mid-year (no names to you), and
a sheet that identifies children by name alone — **that sheet cannot tell them apart, and neither
can anything built from it.** Then Decide 10.9: what is the id, where does it come from, **and
who can issue a new one?**

This is the one decision where you can give a near-answer, and should: **check the SIS first.**
If it already assigns a permanent student id, that's almost certainly it — *one fewer thing to
keep in step.*

**Decide 10.10 — the cut-over.** The second half of case 3, and the only decision that genuinely
needs authority. *Going forward, this is the source of truth for which children are enrolled.*

Give both failure modes, because they're symmetric and people only see one: **too early, before
it's accurate, and you've created one more unreliable list. Never, and you have a hub nobody
trusts — which is the same as not having one.**

Then the three picks: the date, who announces it, how people are told. **If they can't answer
"who says so", they have found the real obstacle — and it is not a spreadsheet.** Say that
plainly and don't soften it into a next step.

## Part C — the one page

**Section 10, and the prompt.** First the honest split: generating ids, making sure none collide,
copying messy records into a registry — **that part you do well**, and Module 11 does exactly
that. But: you cannot decide which source is the source of truth, cannot tell them whether it's
accurate, cannot pick the cut-over date, **and cannot make their colleagues use the thing once it
exists.** *The tools are the easy half.*

Then draft the page from their four answers — written for somebody who has never thought about
this, so they can see what is being asked of them and why. Read it back to them before they take
it anywhere.

## The ending

1. The held question, word for word. The answer, which is the best paragraph in the module:
   **the data was never missing.** It was scattered across sources that disagreed about who the
   children were, in copies nobody had ranked, mixed in with sheets being read and typed into at
   the same time. *Every one of those is a decision nobody made.* They have now made four.
2. Then: *notice what still feels unresolved.* If it's **"I don't have the authority to do
   this"**, that is the most useful thing in the module, and the next conversation is with a
   person rather than a computer. Don't try to fix it for them.
3. Their task list (ten), with the four decisions named as decisions.
4. The assignment: take the one-page summary to whoever owns the roster and ask one question —
   **"is this the source of truth?"** *Their answer tells you how much of this work is technical
   and how much of it is not.*
5. Next: Module 11, *Building the hub* — eleven messy exports from a school that doesn't exist,
   turned into one record per child and three views onto it. **Every decision they just made,
   carried out.** It isn't in the tutor yet; its page is
   `https://dev-portal-byauh7qecq-ue.a.run.app/modules.html`

## What goes wrong, and what to do

The page's table (section 11) is a list of things learners *say*, which makes it unusually
useful — read it before this module, because you'll hear all six. Beyond it:

- **They want you to pick their source of truth.** The module's own failure, performed by their
  tutor. Help them see who's accountable when it's wrong; that person's system is almost always
  the answer.
- **"Our information system is wrong."** First row, and the answer is good: a source of truth
  **doesn't have to be accurate today — it has to be the thing you fix into.** Name it, then make
  its accuracy somebody's job.
- **"I'm not the one who decides."** True for most people doing this module, **and it is the
  actual finding.** Do 10.5 and 10.10 as a proposal, and take it to whoever does.
- **"This feels like a lot of meetings."** Four decisions, and **three of them they can make
  themselves.** Only the cut-over needs authority.
- **They skip the colleagues and answer from memory.** Let them, say what it costs, and offer to
  come back to 10.1 after they've asked two people — it takes ninety seconds and it's the most
  convincing thing in the module.
- **They want to start building the registry now.** Good instinct; that's Module 11, and it's on
  the website. Make sure the four decisions are written down first — *that is the whole reason
  this module comes before that one.*
