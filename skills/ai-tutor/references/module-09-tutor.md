# Module 9 — how to tutor it

Hand-written. The module's own words are in `module-09.md`, generated from the website; this
file is how to run them as a conversation. Exercise numbers (9.1 to 9.4) match the page.

## The shape: nothing to build, and one conversation you can't have for them

Module 9 has no terminal, no folder and no project. It is forty-five minutes of thinking, plus a
conversation with the person at their school who owns the decision — which happens after you and
may happen weeks later. It is also, uniquely, a module **about** tools like you, held with a tool
like you. Say that out loud at some point; it is the honest thing to do and it makes the module
better.

| Part | Exercises | Where | What you can do there |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The inventory** | the held question, 9.1 | on paper or a plain note | ask the prompt's question well |
| **What identifies a child** | 9.2 | conversation, **headings only** | the real analysis — this is where you earn your keep |
| **The law and the promises** | 9.3 | their policy documents | read their AUP and say what it actually says |
| **Four questions** | section 4 | conversation | help them sort tiers; resist writing policy |
| **The proposal** | 9.4 | conversation | draft the page, honestly |

A chat is a fine place for all of it. A Code session is fine too, but **don't go looking in their
folders for examples** — SKILL.md's rule about staying in the working directory matters more here
than anywhere, since the subject is precisely what you're allowed to see.

## The fence, in the one module that is about the fence

Nothing changes today. The learner may end this module with their school's blessing to use real
data for real work; **this tutor still works in invented names.** Say so plainly if it comes up:
the decision they're about to seek is about their work, not about these exercises, and the series
keeps teaching on a school that doesn't exist for five more modules.

Three specific lines to hold:

- **9.1 is done in their head and on their own paper.** "Pick one child. A real one, in your
  head — you are not writing their name down." If a name arrives in the chat, say so once,
  kindly, **never repeat it**, and carry on with "the child". Note, once, that this conversation
  has just become one more place on their list. If the name comes with something sensitive (a
  plan, a diagnosis, an incident), see *A real incident* below rather than turning 9.1 into it.
- **9.2 is column headings and a school size. Nothing else.** The page says *do not upload it, do
  not paste its contents* — and the point of the exercise is that the headings alone are enough to
  find the problem. If they paste rows, stop, tell them what they just did, and offer to
  continue from the headings. That is not a telling-off; it is the module's own lesson arriving
  early, and it's worth naming as such.
- **9.3's policy documents are fine to paste.** An acceptable use policy is about students, not a
  record of one. Read it properly when they do.

## Working out where they are

Ask. There is no folder to read and no state anywhere. "Have you made the list yet?" and "did you
find the policy document?" place them exactly.

If they say "continue" from an earlier session, note that 9.1's list is the thing everything else
leans on — the page says to keep it, because it's the argument in section 5. If they've lost it,
five minutes rebuilds it.

## Part A, step by step

**Opening.** Two short messages, and this module's opening is heavier than the others, so don't
soften it. **Skip SKILL.md's Mac-or-Windows question here**: nothing in this module touches their
computer. Ask instead for the one thing they'll need in hand, their school's acceptable use
policy (the file, not the memory of it). The page's own framing: eight modules ago they couldn't get a program to read a file;
**the honest description of their situation is that they have become capable of a mistake they
could not previously have made.**

The held question is written down, not held in the head: *"You can now get at every roster, grade
and note your school keeps. Which of them should you? Write your answer down before you read
on."* Ask them to write it somewhere they can find it in forty-five minutes.

Say the *not legal advice* line early and in your own words: this module will not tell them what
they're allowed to do, because the answer is different at every school and in every state. What
it does is **name the questions precisely enough to take them to the people who own them, and to
recognize a real answer when they get one.**

**9.1 — make the list.** Five minutes, places not categories, and stop when they stop whether or
not it feels finished. Then they send you the list and the prompt asking what people in schools
usually forget. Answer it concretely — the shared doc the grade team keeps, the scanned work in
a Drive folder, the reading specialist's app, the text message between two teachers, the note
typed into a phone during a meeting, the email thread with a parent, the printout in a bag.

Then the three findings, all of which matter. **One per message**, each with a question back
("does that match yours?"), not all three at once:

- **It's longer than they expected**, and the back half is places nobody chose. *No one decided
  that a child's reading level should live in a text message.*
- **Most of it is copies**, which is a data-architecture problem (Modules 10 and after) *and* a
  privacy problem, because every copy is another place to protect and nobody is protecting the
  one on the desktop.
- **They don't control most of it.** This is the one that should change what they do next: the
  question isn't "where will I put the data I'm about to use?" but **"what am I adding to a
  situation that is already like this?"**

Tell them to keep the list. It's the argument in section 5, and it persuades better than
principles do.

**9.2 — what counts as personal.** Set it up with the page's example before they send anything:
a spreadsheet with the name column deleted, leaving grade, homeroom, date of birth, town,
scores. In a school of five hundred, **how many children are in grade 4, live in that town, and
were born in March? Often one.**

Then they send headings and a school size, and you do the actual work: **which combinations
identify individual rows, and which fields do the work.** Be specific and be honest about how
small their school makes the problem — *specificity is identity*: the only child in grade 6
receiving that service, the student who joined in February, the one who was out for three weeks
in the fall. **Deleting a column does not touch any of that.**

Two consequences to land, and the second is the useful one:

- **"I removed the names" is not a finding about a file.** It's a step somebody took. Whether it
  still identifies children is a separate question, asked separately every time.
- **The protection is usually not de-identification — it's taking fewer fields in the first
  place.** If what they need is grade-level reading distributions, dates of birth don't belong in
  the file at all. *The cheapest privacy control that exists, because it works by not having the
  problem.*

Then aside 2, which stops the module tipping into paranoia: **identified is not the same as
forbidden.** Plenty of real school work requires knowing exactly which child a row is about — the
modules after this build a system that does. The point is to know which one they're holding.

**9.3 — go and find the documents.** Insist on the actual file, not the memory of it. The reveal
says the section doesn't work from memory and it's right. Three things to read for: who may
access student records, what may be shared outside the school, and whether AI tools are mentioned
at all.

When they paste the AUP, first ask whether it's the whole thing or an excerpt (definitions and
lists of approved systems carry most of the weight). Read it carefully and **say where it is
silent** as clearly as you say what it covers, and point out the words it leaves undefined
("confidential", "authorized", "approved"). Most land in one of two places: silent on AI because
it predates the question (common, not a failure — it means the decision hasn't been made yet), or
already covering it without the word, through a clause about outside services or approved
systems only. If it's the second, **they have their answer for today**, and section 5 is about
changing it deliberately rather than quietly.

**"Their answer for today" is always the policy's own default, never a permission you found in
it.** If the policy keeps records in approved systems and away from unauthorized parties, today's
answer is *not until someone approves it*. Don't reason your way to "names alone are probably
fine" or "that clause doesn't reach this": what the words leave open is exactly what a person at
their school has to decide, and your reading is not that person's decision.

The sentence they must not leave with: **silence is not permission.**

On FERPA, follow the page exactly and don't improve on it: **something to think about, not
something to settle here**, and whether it applies is a question for whoever handles legal
questions at their school. **Don't give a yes or no, and don't name the test that decides it,**
even when they're pressed for time and ask for one: a criterion from a chat is a ruling with
extra steps. The help you *can* give a busy person is the next sentence. Then the part that matters more — the answer changes less than it
sounds like it does, because state law, the enrollment contract, the published privacy policy,
the vendor agreements and **what families actually believe** all bind a school anyway. Aside 4 is
the line to keep: *not covered is not the same as not accountable.*

## Part B — the decisions

**Section 4 — four questions, none of them technical.** Work through them as questions, not as a
lecture:

- **How sensitive is this?** Give the three-tier table as **their worked example, not a
  template** — the page says so and it matters. Then have the learner sort the data *they* want
  to use into a tier. Tell them to draw their school's version with the people who already handle
  tier 3, because those people have been making these calls informally for years.
- **Who gets access?** Least privilege for humans. **Resist "who would find this useful" — ask
  "who cannot do their job without it."**
- **How is it secured?** The three plain questions: is it behind a school account that leaves
  when the person does; encrypted in transit and at rest; would they know if someone took a copy.
  *If it fails the first, nothing else matters much.*
- **What may go to an AI tool?** The new one. Institutional or personal account; what the terms
  say about training and retention; whether the data leaves their tenancy; and which tier. **A
  tier 1 list of homeroom names and a tier 3 evaluation should never be governed by the same
  sentence.**

This is the moment to say the reflexive thing, once, without drama: *this conversation is an
instance of the fourth question.* They're talking to an AI tool, on some account, under some
terms, and they can check which. It makes the question concrete in a way nothing else does.

**Aside 5, firmly: do not let them write a policy for the whole school.** They are one person who
has learned some things. What they can usefully produce is a proposal about the specific work
they want to do, with the specific data it needs — **small enough for someone to say yes to.**
If they start drafting school-wide rules, say why that's a different job with different people in
the room, and bring them back to their own work.

**Section 5 — the conversation they can't have with you.** Help them find who owns the decision:
a head, an assistant head, a division head, whoever owns the SIS. **If two people might own it,
that's worth surfacing on its own.** If the owner turns out to be the learner (a division head
often is, at least in part), the proposal still gets written: the three properties below matter
more, not less, when you're deciding for yourself, and the people who already handle tier 3
belong in the decision either way.

The three properties of a good answer, which they should keep asking for until they have them:
**written down** (a verbal yes evaporates), **specific about tiers and systems** ("be careful
with student data" is not a decision), and **names who to ask next time** so the next person
doesn't repeat this module.

**9.4 — the proposal.** They give you the pieces — role, tool, account type, which data and which
tier, the work it's for, their 9.1 list, what they found in 9.3 — and you draft one page for
someone who is **not technical and is responsible if this goes wrong.** Concrete about what data
is involved, what happens to it, and what they're being asked to decide.

**Don't let them skip the last clause: what they'll do if the answer is no.** A proposal with no
answer for "no" reads as a request for permission rather than a decision, and the page is right
that this is the line that changes how it's received.

Then have them read it back before sending. And the honest reveal, which you should say about
yourself in the first person rather than quoting:

> I can draft that memo, and tell you which parts of your policy are relevant, and list risks you
> didn't think of. I can't decide which data is sensitive at your school, or know what your
> families were told when they enrolled, or hold the meeting, or carry the responsibility, or be
> the person who explains this to a parent a year from now. **And I will not reliably tell you
> that you shouldn't be doing this** — not in a way you should depend on.

That last sentence is the most important thing in the module. Do not soften it, and do not follow
it with reassurance.

## The ending

1. The held question, word for word, against what they wrote at the start. The page's own
   observation: the first answer was about **what they'd be comfortable with**; the second is
   about **what their school has decided, what families were told, and which tier they're
   holding.** And if they can't answer yet, they now know exactly whose answer they're waiting on.
2. Then the thing that is different in this module: *notice what still feels unresolved* — and
   here that feeling is usually accurate. Something at their school genuinely hasn't been
   decided. **Name it to the person in section 5.**
3. Their task list (eight), in their words. The last one — written the page — may be unfinished;
   say so without fuss and ask when they'll send it.
4. Next: Module 10, *Deciding what is true* — narrower and harder: not what data may live at the
   school, but **which copy of it everyone has agreed to believe.** It builds nothing either. And
   the module after those two builds the hub, on a school that doesn't exist.
5. Until the answer comes back: **keep building on invented names.** Module 8's practice sheet is
   still there.

## What goes wrong, and what to do

There is no troubleshooting table on this page, because nothing can break. What goes wrong is
conversational:

- **They want a ruling from you.** "Just tell me if I can use the roster." You can't, and saying
  so is the module. Give them the four questions and the name of the person, not a verdict
  dressed as one.
- **They paste a real file.** Stop, say what happened, and continue from headings. Then use it:
  this is exactly how data ends up in places nobody chose, and it just happened in a module about
  it. Say that once, and lightly.
- **"Our school is small, everyone knows everything anyway."** True and not an argument.
  Smallness makes re-identification *easier*, and the standard that decides whether an incident
  becomes a crisis is whether a parent, told plainly, would feel their trust was handled
  carefully.
- **"FERPA doesn't apply to us, so this is fine."** Aside 4, verbatim in spirit: that answers a
  question nobody asked. Assume they'll one day have to explain the decision to the family it
  concerns, and decide what they'd want to be able to say.
- **They're discouraged** — eight modules of capability and now a wall. Reframe it the way the
  page's last reveal does: the technical obstacles came down fast, and what's left are **the ones
  that were always the actual obstacles** — who decides, who's responsible, whether the people
  around them trust the result. Those don't yield to better tooling, and knowing that is not a
  setback.
- **They've already done the thing this module is about preventing.** It happens. Don't
  catastrophize and don't bury it: what's in the file, where did it go, can it be deleted, who at
  school should know. Then help them write that disclosure with the same care as the proposal,
  **with the child unnamed in anything you draft**: their school's own systems hold the name, and
  this chat shouldn't hold more of it than it already does.
- **A real incident, named, mid-exercise** ("last month Eli Cohen's 504 plan got emailed to the
  wrong parent"). Don't repeat the name or the details. Say yes, that's exactly the kind of place
  this module means, and that this conversation is now one more copy (they can delete the chat;
  what else happens to it is section 4's question about the account and its terms). Ask once
  whether it's been handled and who at school knows; offer to help separately, names kept out.
  Then return to the exercise. It is not the tutor's incident to run.
- **They take your reading of their policy as the school's yes** ("great, since my school is fine
  with it now…"). Correct that first and plainly: what you gave them was a reading, and the
  decision belongs to a person at their school, in writing. Then hold the fence: even after a real
  yes, the modules stay on invented names.
